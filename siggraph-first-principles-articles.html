A Surface Is A Promise About What Stays Connected

A Surface Is A Promise About What Stays Connected

Core idea

The burden is connectivity. A mesh, field, or point set must let later code recover which pieces belong together and which side of the object a query lies on.

Why the simple approach fails

The tempting shortcut is to manipulate visible samples independently: move vertices, paint pixels, average frames, trace fewer rays, generate a finished object in one step, or smooth away a bad contact. These moves often improve the visible case while breaking the hidden structure. SIGGRAPH papers are valuable when they show how to make the hidden structure inspectable.

Representative paper groups

geometry-processing-meshes

Geometry Processing Meshes

Ordinary problem. These papers are about making digital shape behave like a trustworthy object instead of a loose collection of samples. A mesh, meaning a surface stored as many small connected triangles or quads, may need to be measured, cleaned, simplified, animated, printed, textured, cut into panels, or compared with another surface. The shared difficulty is that the visible object is continuous, but the computer often holds it as pieces: vertices, faces, rays, fields, slices, correspondences, tokens, or captured points. If the pieces are changed independently, the object can tear, cross itself, lose sharp edges, blur texture, break inside-out tests, or become too slow to query. The family therefore keeps asking the same question in different forms: what relationships must remain true while the representation changes? Some papers preserve appearance while remaking triangles, some preserve distance or winding tests while accelerating queries, some preserve cloth, garment, skeleton, or toolpath structure while adapting shape to motion or manufacture. The common object is not just a shape, but a shape plus the rules that make it usable.

Naive attempt and what breaks. A smart beginner might try to treat every small piece of geometry separately. To compare two surfaces, check every triangle against every other triangle. To clean a tangled surface, push intersecting faces apart locally. To preserve a texture, keep the old image and hope the new triangles still line up. To generate an artist-style surface, list faces one by one. To print a non-planar object, slice it into simple layers and connect nearby paths. These attempts fail because shape errors are relational. A small local fix can create a global wrongness: an inside becomes outside, a sharp feature rounds off, a garment no longer matches a body, a cloth frame no longer agrees with nearby frames, or a toolpath collides after a layer looks valid by itself. Brute force also fails at scale. Exact distance, winding, intersection, and surface evaluation become expensive when every query must inspect too many pieces.

Mathematical object. The recurring principle is to turn shape into constraints that can be measured and preserved while the representation changes. Distance methods measure the worst mismatch between two surfaces, then avoid useless work by proving that whole regions cannot contain the answer. Winding-number methods measure whether a point is inside by adding the signed influence of the surrounding surface, then reduce that addition to faster ray or boundary calculations. Remeshing and offsetting preserve visible appearance, sharp features, and correspondence, meaning the new pieces must still point back to the same parts of the old object. Untangling and panelization adjust vertices or surface flows while penalizing intersections, stress, or loss of fidelity. Generation and capture papers use fields, correspondences, and aggregations to carry hidden structure such as skeletons, skin weights, garment-body matches, wrinkle motion, or surface signals. The math matters because it defines what counts as wrong: too far away, crossing when it should not, losing an edge, changing density, breaking endpoint distributions, or violating toolpath thickness. Once wrongness is measurable, algorithms can search, subdivide, simplify, or stop early with a reason.

Why this math fits. The mathematics is not decoration because surfaces contain structure that the eye, the printer, the simulator, and downstream tools all depend on. A triangle list alone does not say which side is inside, which edge is intentionally sharp, which texture sample belongs to which point, or whether two nearby folds are allowed to touch. Boundary integrals, ray tests, Hausdorff bounds, convex hulls, and Lipschitz limits give ways to make claims about entire pieces of shape instead of guessing from individual samples. Energy terms, which are scores that rise when a surface bends, intersects, drifts, or carries stress badly, let several demands be balanced at once. Differential operators and harmonic maps describe how quantities change across a surface, so smoothing, curvature, uncertainty, and constant-curvature design can be handled as surface behavior rather than image cleanup. Fields are useful because they assign values throughout space or over a domain, which lets printing paths, skeleton confidence, fabric fibers, or generated signals be followed continuously instead of as disconnected decisions.

How the papers split. The representative papers fit together because each protects a different relationship while changing or querying geometry. Texture-Aware Remeshing changes the triangle layout but moves texture information onto vertices so later geometry operations still see appearance. Iskra asks how existing geometry programs can run backward, so a desired output can tell the input shape how to change. The winding-number papers and the Hausdorff-distance papers make inside-out and surface-distance questions faster by replacing exhaustive checks with ray tests, boundary formulas, branch-and-bound, buffers, moments, and certified subdivision. Feature-Preserving Offset Meshing, the NURBS intersection algorithm, Structural MAT, and Subgrid Marching Tetrahedra all guard structural details while deriving new surfaces, intersections, skeleton-like medial descriptions, or fine sheets from imperfect discrete input. Untangling Surfaces and Stress-Aware Panelization score the shape against physical or geometric violations, then adjust it to remove crossings or balance stress and fidelity. Strips as Tokens, Matern Noise, AniGen, Tempo3D, ACT, and virtual try-on bring the same concern into learned generation: the output must carry connections, UV boundaries, skeletons, motion, or garment correspondences, not just look plausible. Cloth capture, fabric capture, kirigami, AtomSlicer, and MorphSkein show the same logic when shape must obey cameras, fibers, cuts, print beads, or pixel density.

What changed in 2026. The 2026 pattern in this evidence is that geometry processing is less isolated from the rest of graphics. Classical questions such as distance, intersection, inside-out tests, remeshing, offsets, medial axes, and curvature are still present, but they are being pushed onto GPUs, made certified, or connected to uncertainty. At the same time, generated and captured shapes are expected to arrive with usable structure: UV boundaries, skeletons, skinning, garment correspondences, cloth motion, fabric fibers, and surface signals. Fabrication and display papers also demand geometry that respects physical constraints such as bead thickness, deployability, stress, collision freedom, and pixel density. The shift is from producing a surface to producing a surface that remains actionable.

Failure boundary. The evidence does not prove a single universal geometry method. Many results depend on assumptions about the input: triangle meshes, NURBS curves and patches, UV parameterization, clean or dirty surfaces, high-resolution meshes, microscopic images, bracketed exposures, planar graphs, or known garment and body proxies. Several approaches rely on discretization, floating-point arithmetic, learned models, or simulators, so their guarantees may weaken outside the tested setting. Speedups for winding numbers, Hausdorff distance, neural surface queries, or hair scattering do not automatically transfer to every model size, tolerance, hardware layout, or visual case. The abstracts also state contributions more than failures. They do not fully establish behavior on extreme self-intersections, missing texture evidence, unusual skeletons, severe capture occlusion, unsupported materials, or fabrication machines with different physical limits.

neural-fields-representations

Neural Fields Representations

Ordinary problem. These papers belong together because they ask how a computer can store something that is spread continuously through space, time, direction, or material, while still answering local questions quickly. The stored thing may be a volume seen by a camera ray, a surface that can be pulled by an artist, a lattice material whose small cells decide whole-part stiffness, a hidden thermal scene mixed by a rough wall, a portrait whose lighting must change over time, or a point cloud that should behave like a solid shape. A direct stored grid is wasteful because most places are simple, empty, smooth, or repeated, while a few places carry sharp edges, contacts, lighting changes, or mechanical detail. A plain learned rule can be compact, but it may be slow to ask at every point and hard to edit. The family problem is therefore representation under pressure: keep enough hidden structure to recover surfaces, images, forces, color, or motion, but avoid storing or querying every possible sample.

Naive attempt and what breaks. A smart beginner would choose one uniform description and push it hard. Store a dense table of values everywhere. March a ray in tiny equal steps. Ask the same neural network at every point. Smooth every surface because smooth functions are easy to learn. Fit soft blobs to a volume and draw them all at every viewing scale. Or let a learned surrogate replace a physics solver without respecting the solver's coarse-to-fine structure. These attempts fail because the world is uneven. Empty space and flat regions do not need the same work as a sharp boundary. Smooth models blur jumps and corners. Uniform sampling wastes effort away from surfaces and misses thin features if the step size is too large. A fast learned material solver can be useless if its errors are unstable by engineering standards.

Mathematical object. The recurring mathematical principle is adaptive representation: describe a continuous object with smaller pieces whose cost follows the local difficulty of the question. In plain terms, each method builds a map that can be asked, what is here, what direction matters here, how much detail is needed here, or can this region be skipped. Gabor Fields use oriented wave-like filters, meaning small directional pattern detectors, so volume detail can be kept or pruned by viewing scale and direction. SAND lets easy spatial locations stop early when a learned surface rule has already answered enough. Bounding stratified Bernoulli impulses groups random yes-or-no surface events into regions, then uses bounds to avoid marching through points that cannot change the result. NeuBase puts learned offsets on top of a smooth editable base surface, so deformation has local handles instead of becoming a black box. GMT follows multigrid logic, where a hard physical solve is handled from coarse structure to fine correction. Across the family, wrongness means lost detail, slow queries, broken edit behavior, unstable residual error, missed surfaces, or a mismatch to measured images, materials, or physical response.

Why this math fits. The math matters because the underlying signals are not random piles of numbers. Surfaces usually change smoothly until they reach an edge or branch. Volume detail has scale and direction: a broad cloudy region and a thin stripe should not cost the same. A point cloud gives scattered evidence of a surface, but nearby points and local curvature help decide inside, outside, offsets, and Boolean shape operations. Composite layers and lattice microstructures have repeated small structure whose whole-object stiffness can be predicted only if the small cells are averaged in a controlled way. Light, color, and thermal measurements are mixtures, so a method must separate causes before it can relight, reconstruct, or display them. Adaptive depth, bounds, basis functions, multigrid levels, sparse singularities, tensor factors, and local torus fits are different bookkeeping tools for the same tradeoff: compact storage versus trustworthy recovery. They work when the chosen structure matches the real regularity in the scene, material, surface, sensor, or physical system.

How the papers split. The representative papers are siblings because each makes a continuous field more queryable without treating every location alike. Gabor Fields, Soft Anisotropic Diagrams, and neural quadrature all decide where detail or samples deserve spending: by orientation and scale for volumes, by soft spatial cells for images, or by learned sample placement for integration. SAND, the Bernoulli-impulse ray marcher, DCx, Points as Tori, and SharpNet all focus on surfaces whose answers must be fast but cannot erase sharp or unusual structure; they differ in whether they skip easy regions, extract an explicit surface, fit local distance shapes, or add controlled jumps to a learned function. NeuBase and explicit flows make learned shape descriptions editable by giving them base surfaces or invertible motion paths. GMT and the laminated-composite paper move the same idea into engineering: represent physical response or manufacturable layers as fields that can be solved or adjusted efficiently. EgoRelight, Pixel Cube, PAColorHolo, thermal hidden-scene imaging, Schlieren flow reconstruction, and material modeling show the imaging branch, where the field stores appearance, light, color, flow, or uncertainty so new views, lighting, or reconstructions can be asked for later.

What changed in 2026. The 2026 pattern is that neural fields are being judged less as clever compact containers and more as working parts inside editing, rendering, capture, and engineering systems. The evidence emphasizes fast queries, early exits, region bounds, direct deformation, explicit surface extraction, controlled sharp features, manufacturable layers, and solver-level accuracy. Another change is selectivity. Rather than using the same network depth, sample pattern, or volume detail everywhere, many papers adapt computation to local complexity, direction, scale, uncertainty, or perceptual importance. The family is also spreading beyond view synthesis into materials, holographic color, hidden thermal imaging, flow reconstruction, pose, motion retargeting, radiation pressure, and composite design.

Failure boundary. The evidence does not prove a universal representation for all geometry, light, material, motion, and physics. Many methods depend on the field having the kind of structure their shortcut expects: directional volume detail, locally smooth surfaces, reliable bounds, editable subdivision bases, sparse lattice hierarchies, usable point normals, recoverable lighting, or training examples close to the test case. Adaptive methods can fail if they skip a rare feature that later matters. Smooth learned functions still need special handling for discontinuities, branches, or non-manifold cases, meaning places where a surface connection is not the simple sheet-like case. Solver surrogates must keep residual errors within task requirements, not just look plausible. The abstracts report contributions and selected numbers, but not complete failure behavior under severe noise, extreme topology, unusual materials, unsupported sensors, or deployment constraints.

Simulation Works By Balancing What Cannot Be Violated

Core idea

The burden is conservation and constraint. The next state must pay for mass, force, contact, volume, bend, and coupling rather than simply looking smooth.

Why the simple approach fails

The tempting shortcut is to manipulate visible samples independently: move vertices, paint pixels, average frames, trace fewer rays, generate a finished object in one step, or smooth away a bad contact. physical-simulation

Physical Simulation

Ordinary problem. These papers belong together because they all try to make a digital world obey ordinary physical expectations while staying fast enough to use. Sand should settle instead of vanishing, water should push air and grains aside, a thin sheet should bend without looking rubbery, yarn should hold loops, cloth should match a moving body, wood decay should follow grain and moisture, and a robot motion should respect balance and contact. The shared problem is that real matter is continuous and crowded, while the computer must break it into points, cells, particles, bodies, samples, or learned shortcuts. Each small piece has local rules, but the viewer notices global failures: lost volume, jitter, blur, self-crossing, wrong bending, unstable contact, or motion that no real body could perform. The family is therefore about carrying physical bookkeeping through change. It asks what must be preserved as time advances: mass, volume, momentum, contact separation, bend resistance, heat exchange, force from light, or agreement between a captured or designed object and how it could actually move.

Naive attempt and what breaks. A smart beginner might animate the visible surface directly: move vertices where the artist wants them, blur colors to show mixing, push interpenetrating objects apart, or take bigger time steps to make the scene faster. For fluids, they might store water, air, and sand on one grid and hope the parts share space cleanly. For cloth or shells, they might penalize stretching and add a simple bending score. For robots or garments, they might copy motion from one body to another. These ideas fail because physical mistakes accumulate. A tiny loss of volume becomes draining water or shrinking sand. A local contact fix can create new intersections nearby. Large time steps skip over collisions or smear free surfaces. A copied motion can put a foot through the floor or ask a joint to do impossible work. The hard part is not moving things; it is moving them while honoring many coupled rules at once.

Mathematical object. The recurring math idea is to turn physical behavior into quantities that can be checked, balanced, and carried forward through time. Some papers add up many small effects: random photon paths become radiation pressure, many possible heat paths become a temperature boundary answer, and many fluid or sand samples become bulk motion. Some split a hard problem into easier agreements: yarn constraints, contact rules, body joints, or distributed regions are solved separately and then forced to match. Some search for settings that reduce wrongness, where wrongness means lost volume, mismatched captured cloth, unbalanced load across machines, a body drifting from a reference motion, or a shape crossing itself. Some replace expensive detail with a smaller but faithful summary: moment-space flow, low-rank deformation modes, fixed integration points, precomputed matrices, or a learned proxy that answers force queries quickly. The plain principle is conservation plus correction. Conservation says certain totals or relationships should not disappear when the clock ticks. Correction says that when the computer's rough pieces disagree, the method measures the disagreement and pushes the pieces back toward a physically believable state.

Why this math fits. The math matters because physical scenes have hidden structure that a picture alone does not contain. Water and sand can look like a moving boundary, but underneath they exchange volume and push on each other. A shell can look like a surface, but its bending depends on how nearby patches rotate, not just where points sit. Knitwear can look like cloth, but each yarn loop constrains neighboring loops. A terrain river is shaped by rare transport events over long times, not just one local velocity. Contact needs inequality rules: two things may touch, but their interiors should not occupy the same place. Time stepping needs stability rules: the next frame should not create more energy, mass loss, or visual noise just because the step was large. These papers exploit structure in the world: matter is locally connected, forces add up, constraints repeat, slow and fast processes can be separated, and many systems can be summarized without tracking every microscopic detail. That is why counting, sampling, partitioning, solving, and correction are central rather than decorative.

How the papers split. The representative papers show the same family logic across very different materials. Air-water-sand coupling, two-phase flow, fast free-surface flow, high-order fluid flow, VEM fluids, Mixwell, and terrain erosion all track how material moves through space without losing sharp boundaries, volume, or long-time structure. Better Bending, yarn-level knitwear, cloth capture, virtual try-on, origami materials, and deformation models ask how thin or soft things can bend, fold, wrinkle, or fit bodies while respecting contact and material limits. YASPS, M-ABD, heterogeneous subspace corrections, MPM Lite, JGS2-GQ, and low-rank Koopman deformables are about making large physical solves cheaper by reusing structure, splitting work, or shrinking the number of active unknowns. Radiation pressure, neutron transport, holography, time-of-flight rendering, and nonlinear heat boundaries add many uncertain paths of light, particles, or heat, then use sampling or learned shortcuts to make design questions practical. Surface untangling and distributed partitioning protect the surrounding pipeline: if shape crosses itself or work is divided badly, later physical steps fail. Even procedural deforming texture belongs here because it tries to keep surface detail physically plausible under extreme stretching without running a full physical solve.

What changed in 2026. The 2026 version of this family is not just chasing more detailed motion. The evidence points to methods that keep physical bookkeeping while reducing cost: larger time steps, lower memory, fewer active particles, precomputed matrices, smaller deformation summaries, and GPU-friendly formulas. It also shows more crossing between physical animation, capture, design, robotics, rendering, and fabrication. A solver may now support virtual try-on, cloth capture, spacecraft force design, nuclear shielding, haptic proxy choice, or distributed film production. Another change is concern for extensibility and verification: bending models are compared systematically, contact derivatives are generated symbolically, and partitioning includes temporal consistency. The emphasis is shifting from isolated demos toward physical answers that can survive inside larger workflows.

Failure boundary. The evidence does not prove that one physical method handles all materials or scales. Many papers assume a particular representation, such as grids, particles, affine bodies, yarn curves, shell patches, captured cloth templates, crease patterns, or proxy human bodies. Sampling methods depend on enough samples to tame noise. Learned shortcuts depend on training cases or proxy models staying close to the new query. Large time steps, reduced summaries, and fixed integration points can miss small events if their assumptions break. Contact and coupling methods also rely on numerical tolerances and clean enough input. The abstracts report contributions, not full failure maps, so they do not establish behavior on every extreme splash, knot, fracture, garment layer, robot body, lighting condition, machine layout, or biological material.

Fluids Smoke Granular

Ordinary problem. These papers belong together because they all ask how loose, flowing matter can be moved by a computer without losing the physical bookkeeping that makes it believable. Water should carry sand, soak into it, and erode it. Air and water should push on each other across a changing boundary. A painted brush stroke should stir colors sharply instead of turning them into blur. A large flow should keep small swirling detail while using memory a normal machine can afford. A controlled fluid scene should also be adjustable, which means the computer must know how a change made now affects motion many steps later. The shared pressure is that fluids and grains are not single solid objects. Their visible shape comes from many tiny exchanges of volume, push, drift, settling, and mixing. The computer has to chop that continuous motion into cells, particles, samples, or stored displacement rules, then make those pieces agree as time advances. The family is about making that chopping useful without letting it leak water, smear edges, erase vortices, or make design changes too expensive to trace.

Naive attempt and what breaks. A smart beginner might draw the surface of the water, slide sand particles downhill, blur colors under a brush, or run one general fluid grid and hope every substance can share it. They might also make the time steps larger to gain speed, or store every detail needed by a high-quality flow method to avoid visual loss. These ideas fail in different-looking but related ways. If water, air, and sand are not coupled carefully, sand can move without the right fluid force, water can vanish from pores, or dry grains can behave like soaked sticky material. If paint mixing is repeatedly resampled, each stroke softens the image. If a fast solver throws away too much state, small swirls disappear. If a control method traces every possible backward influence through a long flow, memory and time explode before the scene can be tuned.

Mathematical object. The recurring mathematical idea is to track what must be carried through motion, then choose a compact way to update it. For sand-water-air mixtures, the carried quantities include fluid volume, grain motion, and water retained inside the granular structure. One method updates fluid behavior with a lattice Boltzmann method, which means it stores small directional flow summaries at grid sites rather than following every molecule, and couples that to a material point method, which means sand is tracked by moving sample points that carry material state. For gas-kinetic fluid solvers, the idea is similar but more compressed: instead of storing many directional distributions, the method evolves moments, meaning bulk summaries such as momentum that describe the crowd behavior of many tiny moving particles. For two-phase flow, the solver must carry both fluid motion and a phase field, a smooth marker telling which liquid is where near the interface. For Mixwell, the stored object is a displacement rule: how an ideal brush tine moves nearby paint. For DiffSurFlow, the stored object is a flow map, a record of how positions travel through time, used to preserve the main routes by which changes affect later motion.

Why this math fits. The math matters because flowing materials hide their important structure inside motion, not just shape. A water surface may look like a boundary, but the believable result depends on volume not disappearing, forces passing between phases, and density differences staying stable. Sand may look like grains, but wet sand changes character when liquid enters and remains inside it. A brush stroke may look like a local smear, but sharp mixing depends on moving pigment along clean paths without repeatedly sampling a texture and softening it. A turbulent flow may look random, yet it contains long-lived swirls and paths that carry information across many frames. The repeated mathematical move is therefore conservation plus selective memory. Conservation says that quantities such as volume, momentum, phase identity, and material state should survive a time step unless the modeled physics changes them. Selective memory says the computer should keep the summaries that determine visible behavior and discard redundant detail. That tradeoff, detail versus speed and memory, is the reason these methods use coupled updates, moment summaries, phase markers, displacement functions, and flow-map shortcuts.

How the papers split. The representative papers are variations on the same deeper move: keep the right history of moving matter while avoiding an impossible amount of bookkeeping. Volume-Preserving LBM-MPM Coupling handles the most literal mixture. It lets air and water evolve as fluids while sand moves as granular material, then makes them exchange forces and preserve volume so settling, transport, erosion, and basin failure do not look like unrelated effects. Kinetic Predicted-Moment Flux Reconstruction and the Extended Full GKS two-phase solver attack the cost of high-quality flow. Both use gas-kinetic reasoning, meaning they describe fluid motion through aggregate particle behavior, but they focus on memory-light updates that still keep fine swirls or sharp two-liquid behavior. Mixwell takes the same concern into 2D painting: instead of solving a full fluid problem after every stroke, it composes brush-induced displacement fields so progressive mixing stays sharp and resolution independent. DiffSurFlow turns the family around from prediction to control. It asks which parts of a long motion carry useful sensitivity backward, then keeps the flow-map connections that act as main influence routes while pruning less useful backward work. Across them, the sibling logic is not the visible material; it is faithful transport under limited computation.

What changed in 2026. The 2026 pattern is a push toward fluid methods that are not merely detailed, but practical under hard limits. The evidence emphasizes lower memory, GPU-friendly steps, progressive editing, and long-horizon control. Several papers avoid storing heavy per-point state by using moment summaries, explicit gas-kinetic updates, or displacement functions that compose without intermediate blur. The air-water-sand work adds stronger coupling across materials rather than treating each substance as a separate effect. DiffSurFlow shows another shift: fluids are becoming things to tune and design, not only forward animations to watch. The common change is keeping physically meaningful structure while cutting the cost that usually prevents large scenes, high resolution, or repeated adjustment.

Failure boundary. The evidence does not show a universal fluid method. Each approach depends on its chosen representation: grid-site flow summaries, moving sand samples, phase markers, analytical brush displacement rules, or flow maps for backward influence. Those choices help when their assumptions match the scene, but can fail outside them. Idealized brush tines may not cover every real paint tool. Moment-based gas-kinetic solvers rely on their summaries preserving the flow details that matter. Two-phase solvers must handle interfaces, density contrasts, and boundaries within their stated models. Coupled sand-water-air behavior assumes the water retention and volume conservation models are adequate. DiffSurFlow assumes the retained flow-map connections really carry the important long-range effects. The abstracts support speed, memory, and example behavior claims, but not every extreme splash, sediment case, turbulent state, brush pattern, or control task.
